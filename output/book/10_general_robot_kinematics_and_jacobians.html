10  General Robot Kinematics and Jacobians

Prerequisites and assumptions

The reader should understand vectors, matrices, rank, null spaces, and constraints from Chapter 1; coordinate frames and cross products from Chapter 2; spatial poses, angular velocity, twists, and the adjoint from Chapter 7; and screw axes, the \(SE(3)\) exponential and logarithm, and space-versus-body pose integration from Chapter 8.

This chapter treats fixed-base open serial chains with ideal rigid links and independent one-degree-of-freedom revolute or prismatic joints. Frames are right-handed and orthonormal. Position and length are measured in metres, time in seconds, and angles in radians. The chapter develops forward and velocity kinematics; it does not yet solve inverse kinematics or model joint compliance, backlash, closed kinematic loops, collision, forces, actuator dynamics, or feedback control.

From one joint to a complete robot

Chapter 8 answered a local geometric question: what finite displacement or instantaneous velocity field is produced by one declared screw motion? A serial robot creates a larger problem because several joints move several connected links. The end effector is the physical tool or final working link selected to perform the robot’s task, and an end-effector frame is a coordinate frame rigidly attached to it. Predicting the pose of that frame requires the effects of all upstream joints to be combined in their physical chain order.

Consider a simple arm consisting of a fixed base, a shoulder joint, an upper-arm link, an elbow joint, a forearm link, and a tool frame. Rotating the shoulder moves the upper arm and carries the elbow, forearm, and tool with it. Rotating the elbow changes the forearm and tool relative to their new placement after the shoulder motion, but it does not move the upper arm. The two joint effects therefore cannot be calculated as unrelated pose changes and added: each downstream motion starts from the placement produced by the joints before it.

At one instant, suppose the kinematic model is given one signed coordinate for each of the robot’s \(n\) independent joints. Collect these values in the joint-coordinate column

\[ \mathbf q = \begin{bmatrix} q_1&\cdots&q_n \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \in\mathbb R^n, \]

where a revolute entry is an angle in radians and a prismatic entry is a displacement in metres. The complete kinematic problem begins with three practical questions.

  1. Pose: Where is the selected end-effector frame for the current value of \(\mathbf q\)? Forward kinematics answers this question by composing the link and joint geometry into an end-effector pose.

  2. Velocity: If the joints move with rate column \(\dot{\mathbf q}\), what is the instantaneous velocity of the end effector or another selected task output? A Jacobian is the local linear map that converts joint rates into that selected velocity.

  3. Available motion: Can the joints produce every desired instantaneous task direction at the current configuration? The rank of the relevant Jacobian answers this question. A kinematic singularity for a declared task is a configuration at which that Jacobian loses rank and at least one normally available instantaneous task direction is lost.

These questions form a dependency chain. Forward kinematics must first define how joint coordinates determine a pose or another task output. Differentiating that forward relationship produces velocity kinematics and its Jacobian. Analysing the Jacobian then reveals available motion directions, joint motions that produce no first-order task change, and singular configurations. Inverse kinematics, trajectory generation, and control use these results later; they cannot be stated reliably before the forward map and its derivative are understood.

Chapter 8 supplies the transformation, screw-axis, exponential, logarithm, adjoint, and twist tools for individual rigid motions. This chapter assembles those tools at robot scale. It first distinguishes complete configurations, joint-coordinate descriptions, and selected task outputs. It then constructs serial-chain poses using transform chains and products of exponentials, derives task and rigid-body Jacobians, and finishes by examining rank, singularities, numerical conditioning, and verification checks.

10.1 Configuration coordinates and forward kinematics

The motivation above identified the robot-level forward problem: given one value for every joint coordinate, where is the selected end-effector frame or task point? This section first clarifies the coordinate input, then defines the forward output, and finally differentiates that input–output relationship. It does not yet choose a transform-chain or product-of-exponentials formula for evaluating the forward map; those constructions begin in Section 10.2.

10.1.1 Configuration space and coordinate vectors

The robot’s configuration space \(\mathcal C\) was defined in Section 8.1 as the set of all complete material-point placements that satisfy the rigid-link and joint constraints. A numerical calculation cannot pass an entire placement map \(\chi:\mathcal B\rightarrow\mathbb R^3\) as a convenient input. It instead uses the generalised-coordinate column

\[ \mathbf q = \begin{bmatrix} q_1&\cdots&q_n \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \in\mathcal Q, \]

where \(n\in\mathbb N\) is the number of independent scalar coordinates and \(\mathcal Q\) is their declared domain. The model geometry, joint definitions, and zero arrangements supply the rule \(\mathbf q\mapsto\chi(\mathbf q)\); the entries of \(\mathbf q\) do not themselves contain the poses of all the links.

For example, consider a fixed-base two-joint chain whose first joint is revolute and whose second joint is prismatic. Its coordinate column may be

\[ \mathbf q = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_1\\d_2 \end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(\theta_1\) is measured in radians and \(d_2\) is measured in metres. The column states one rotation amount and one translation amount. It becomes a complete robot configuration only after the kinematic model uses those values to place every link.

The coordinate domain records the allowed value of each joint. For the independent one-degree-of-freedom joints considered here, it has a product form

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal Q = \mathcal Q_1\times\cdots\times\mathcal Q_n }, \]

where \(\mathcal Q_j\) is the domain associated with coordinate \(q_j\). Before a numerical angle representative is chosen, an unrestricted revolute joint contributes the circle

\[ S^1 \mathrel{:=} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} c\\s \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb R^2 \;\middle|\; c^2+s^2=1 \right\}. \]

The angle \(\theta\) represents the point \([\cos\theta\;\sin\theta]^{\mathsf T}\in S^1\), so angles separated by \(2\pi\) represent the same point on the circle and the same joint rotation. Software normally stores a real angle representative together with an explicit wrapping convention. A joint-limited revolute coordinate is represented on its declared angular interval, and a prismatic coordinate is represented on a declared interval in metres or on \(\mathbb R\) in an ideal unbounded model. Thus the global domain \(\mathcal Q\) need not be an ordinary subset of \(\mathbb R^n\), even though every local numerical coordinate column belongs to a subset of \(\mathbb R^n\). Collision constraints could remove combinations from this product, but collision is outside the present model.

This distinction explains why a coordinate column is not the same object as a configuration. The three related objects are

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal C &=\text{set of admissible complete robot placements},\\ \mathcal Q &=\text{declared domain of the coordinate descriptions},\\ \mathbf q &=\text{one coordinate description in }\mathcal Q. \end{aligned} } \]

Coordinates need only describe configurations locally without ambiguity. The reason is visible for one revolute joint: \(\theta\) and \(\theta+2\pi\) are different real-number representatives of the same physical rotation. No single real interval can represent the circle \(S^1\) both continuously and without duplicating a boundary orientation.

To state the local relationship precisely, choose a set \(\mathcal U\subseteq\mathcal C\) of nearby configurations that does not cross a coordinate wrap or another representation boundary, and choose a corresponding open coordinate set \(\mathcal V\subseteq\mathbb R^n\). Here open means that every column in \(\mathcal V\) has a sufficiently small neighbourhood that remains inside \(\mathcal V\). A coordinate chart on \(\mathcal U\) is a smooth one-to-one and onto map

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol\psi:\mathcal U\rightarrow\mathcal V, \qquad \chi\longmapsto \boldsymbol\psi(\chi) \mathrel{:=} \mathbf q }. \]

The word chart means that each configuration in this local set receives one coordinate column, each column in \(\mathcal V\) describes one configuration in \(\mathcal U\), and both directions vary smoothly. Here smoothly means that the coordinate formulas and their inverses have the continuous derivatives required by the calculation. The inverse map gives the local coordinate-to-configuration direction

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol\psi^{-1}:\mathcal V\rightarrow\mathcal U, \qquad \mathbf q\longmapsto \boldsymbol\psi^{-1}(\mathbf q) = \chi(\mathbf q) }. \]

The earlier shorthand \(\mathbf q\mapsto\chi(\mathbf q)\) refers to this direction. A complete software model may use real-valued joint columns outside one chart and then apply explicit wrapping and joint-limit rules; those rules are part of the representation contract rather than new physical degrees of freedom.

When the robot moves, its coordinate column becomes a time-dependent curve

\[ \mathbf q:I\rightarrow\mathcal Q, \qquad t\longmapsto\mathbf q(t), \]

where \(I\subseteq\mathbb R\) is a time interval measured in seconds. At a time \(t\) where the coordinate curve is differentiable, its generalised-rate column is

\[ \boxed{ \dot{\mathbf q}(t) \mathrel{:=} \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf q}{\mathrm dt}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \dot q_1(t)&\cdots&\dot q_n(t) \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} }. \]

A revolute entry of \(\dot{\mathbf q}\) is measured in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), while a prismatic entry is measured in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Thus neither \(\mathbf q\) nor \(\dot{\mathbf q}\) should be assigned one common physical unit when the chain contains both joint types. The rate column describes how the chosen coordinates change; it is not yet the velocity of any material point or frame.

10.1.2 Task variables and the forward map

The complete configuration \(\chi(\mathbf q)\) locates every modelled material point, but a calculation usually asks for a smaller output. Examples include the pose of an end-effector frame, the position of a tool point, or the distance between two selected points. A task output is the particular configuration-dependent quantity chosen for the calculation.

For the selected end-effector frame \(\{e\}\) and fixed space frame \(\{s\}\), Section 8.1 defined the forward-pose map

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F_e:\mathcal Q\rightarrow SE(3), \qquad \mathbf q\longmapsto \mathbf F_e(\mathbf q) \mathrel{:=} \mathbf T_{se}(\mathbf q) }. \]

The map \(\mathbf F_e\) is the rule for all admissible coordinate inputs. Its value \(\mathbf T_{se}(\mathbf q)\) is one homogeneous transformation matrix: it represents the pose of frame \(\{e\}\) relative to frame \(\{s\}\) at the selected coordinate column \(\mathbf q\). Forward kinematics solves the direction from joint coordinates to pose; it does not solve which joint coordinates produce a requested pose.

A task may use only part of this pose. Define the position-extraction map

\[ \pi_p:SE(3)\rightarrow\mathbb R^3, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf R&\mathbf p\\ \mathbf 0_3^{\mathsf T}&1 \end{bmatrix} \longmapsto \pi_p\!\left( \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf R&\mathbf p\\ \mathbf 0_3^{\mathsf T}&1 \end{bmatrix} \right) \mathrel{:=} \mathbf p. \]

The end-effector-origin position task is then the composition

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf f_p \mathrel{:=} \pi_p\circ\mathbf F_e: \mathcal Q\rightarrow\mathbb R^3, \qquad \mathbf q\longmapsto {}^{s}\mathbf p_e(\mathbf q) }. \]

Every output column \({}^{s}\mathbf p_e(\mathbf q)\) is expressed in frame \(\{s\}\) and measured in metres. The pose map and the position-task map answer different questions: \(\mathbf F_e\) returns both orientation and position, whereas \(\mathbf f_p\) discards orientation deliberately.

More generally, let \(m\in\mathbb N\) be the number of scalar task coordinates and let \(\mathcal Y\subseteq\mathbb R^m\) be their declared coordinate domain. A task-coordinate map is a function

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf f:\mathcal Q\rightarrow\mathcal Y, \qquad \mathbf q\longmapsto \mathbf y \mathrel{:=} \mathbf f(\mathbf q) }. \]

Here \(\mathbf y=[y_1\;\cdots\;y_m]^{\mathsf T}\) is one task-coordinate column. Each scalar component \(y_i\) is a task variable, also called a task coordinate: it is one declared numerical component of the selected task output and depends on \(\mathbf q\) through \(y_i=f_i(\mathbf q)\). The task variables are outputs of the forward map, not additional independent robot coordinates. Their units, reference frames, and physical meanings must be declared. The dimension \(m\) is chosen by the task and need not equal the number \(n\) of generalised coordinates.

The full modelling chain is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf q \longmapsto \chi(\mathbf q) \longmapsto \mathbf T_{se}(\mathbf q) \longmapsto \mathbf y(\mathbf q) }. \]

The last arrow is present only when the selected task is obtained from the end-effector pose. Some tasks depend on another link, several points, or the complete configuration instead. In every case, the final coordinate rule can still be written as \(\mathbf y=\mathbf f(\mathbf q)\) after the physical quantity and its coordinate representation have been declared.

Consider a planar arm with two revolute joints, link lengths \(L_1,L_2\in\mathbb R_{>0}\) measured in metres, and joint coordinates

\[ \mathbf q = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_1\\\theta_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The first joint axis passes through \(O_s\), both joint axes point along positive \(z_s\), and the end-effector origin is at the distal end of link 2. In the zero arrangement, both links point along positive \(x_s\). The coordinate \(\theta_1\) rotates link 1 relative to the fixed base, while \(\theta_2\) rotates link 2 relative to link 1. Consequently, link 1 makes angle \(\theta_1\) with positive \(x_s\) and link 2 makes the accumulated angle \(\theta_1+\theta_2\) with positive \(x_s\). The position of the end-effector origin in the \(x_s\)–\(y_s\) plane is therefore the sum of the two link-displacement columns:

\[ \begin{aligned} {}^{s}\boldsymbol\ell_1(\theta_1) &= L_1 \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1\\ \sin\theta_1 \end{bmatrix},\\ {}^{s}\boldsymbol\ell_2(\theta_1,\theta_2) &= L_2 \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \({}^{s}\boldsymbol\ell_1\) and \({}^{s}\boldsymbol\ell_2\) are the first- and second-link displacement columns, expressed in frame \(\{s\}\) and measured in metres. Adding them gives \({}^{s}\mathbf p_e={}^s\boldsymbol\ell_1+{}^s\boldsymbol\ell_2\) and therefore the two-coordinate position task

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf y = \mathbf f_{xy}(\mathbf q) = \begin{bmatrix} x_e(\mathbf q)\\ y_e(\mathbf q) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} L_1\cos\theta_1+L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ L_1\sin\theta_1+L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb R^2 }. \tag{10.1}\]

Both entries of \(\mathbf y\) are expressed in frame \(\{s\}\) and measured in metres. If orientation also matters, the planar end-effector angle is

\[ \phi_e(\mathbf q) = \theta_1+\theta_2, \]

and a separate planar pose-coordinate task could use \([x_e\;y_e\;\phi_e]^{\mathsf T}\). That column has two entries in metres and one in radians; it is a coordinate description of a planar pose, not a spatial position vector.

Different task choices preserve different information. Two joint configurations can produce the same end-effector position while giving different link placements or end-effector orientations. Therefore a task-coordinate map need not be one-to-one, and knowing \(\mathbf y\) need not recover \(\mathbf q\). This chapter first solves and differentiates the forward direction; inverse kinematics is developed later in the learning programme.

10.1.3 The total derivative and multivariable chain rule

The forward map answers where the task output is at one configuration. Velocity kinematics asks the local follow-up question: if every joint coordinate changes at a known rate, how does the selected task output change at that instant? The required mathematical object is the total derivative of the task-coordinate map.

Let \(\mathbf f:\mathcal V\rightarrow\mathbb R^m\) be a task-coordinate map on a local coordinate set \(\mathcal V\subseteq\mathbb R^n\), and write its scalar component functions as

\[ \mathbf f(\mathbf q) = \begin{bmatrix} f_1(\mathbf q)&\cdots&f_m(\mathbf q) \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}. \]

Let \(\mathbf q_0\in\mathcal V\) be an interior coordinate value, meaning that sufficiently small positive and negative changes of every coordinate remain inside \(\mathcal V\). The partial derivative of output component \(f_i\) with respect to input coordinate \(q_j\) changes only \(q_j\) while holding the other coordinates fixed:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial q_j}(\mathbf q_0) \mathrel{:=} \lim_{h\rightarrow0} \frac{ f_i(\mathbf q_0+h\mathbf e_j)-f_i(\mathbf q_0) }{h} }. \]

Here \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) is an output index, \(j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) is an input index, \(\mathbf e_j\in\mathbb R^n\) is the dimensionless standard coordinate column with entry \(1\) at index \(j\) and zeros elsewhere, and the scalar increment \(h\) has the physical unit of \(q_j\). The derivative has the unit of \(f_i\) divided by the unit of \(q_j\).

Partial derivatives describe one input direction at a time. The total derivative combines all first-order input effects into one linear map. Its matrix in the chosen input and output coordinates is

\[ \boxed{ \mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q_0) \mathrel{:=} \left[ \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial q_j}(\mathbf q_0) \right]_{ \substack{i=1,\ldots,m\\j=1,\ldots,n} } \in\mathbb R^{m\times n} }. \tag{10.2}\]

Row \(i\) collects the first-order change of task component \(y_i\) caused by every coordinate, while column \(j\) collects the first-order change of every task component caused by coordinate \(q_j\). Because different inputs and outputs may have different units, different entries of this matrix may also have different units.

The defining property of differentiability is that, for a sufficiently small coordinate increment \(\Delta\mathbf q\), the output increment separates into a linear first-order part and a smaller remainder:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf f(\mathbf q_0+\Delta\mathbf q) -\mathbf f(\mathbf q_0) = \mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q_0)\Delta\mathbf q +\mathbf r(\Delta\mathbf q) }, \tag{10.3}\]

where the remainder \(\mathbf r(\Delta\mathbf q)\) decreases faster than the first-order increment as \(\Delta\mathbf q\rightarrow\mathbf 0_n\).1 Thus \(\mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q_0)\) is the local linear model of the generally nonlinear forward map, not a claim that \(\mathbf f\) is globally linear.

Now let \(\mathbf q(t)\) be a differentiable coordinate curve and define the time-dependent task output by

\[ \mathbf y(t) \mathrel{:=} \mathbf f(\mathbf q(t)). \]

Over a small time increment \(\Delta t\), differentiability of the coordinate curve gives

\[ \mathbf q(t+\Delta t) = \mathbf q(t) +\dot{\mathbf q}(t)\Delta t +\mathbf r_q(\Delta t), \]

where the coordinate remainder satisfies \(\mathbf r_q(\Delta t)/\Delta t\rightarrow\mathbf 0_n\) component by component as \(\Delta t\rightarrow0\). Each component of \(\mathbf r_q\) has the same unit as its corresponding coordinate. Substitute this coordinate change into the first-order model Equation 10.3. The corresponding task change has the form

\[ \mathbf y(t+\Delta t)-\mathbf y(t) = \mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q(t)) \dot{\mathbf q}(t)\Delta t +\mathbf r_y(\Delta t), \]

where \(\mathbf r_y(\Delta t)/\Delta t\rightarrow\mathbf 0_m\) component by component and each component of \(\mathbf r_y\) has the same unit as its corresponding task output. Dividing by \(\Delta t\) and taking the limit \(\Delta t\rightarrow0\) makes the remainder quotient vanish and gives the multivariable chain rule along the robot motion:

\[ \boxed{ \dot{\mathbf y}(t) = \mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q(t)) \dot{\mathbf q}(t) }. \tag{10.4}\]

In scalar component form, the same rule is

\[ \boxed{ \dot y_i(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial q_j}(\mathbf q(t)) \dot q_j(t), \qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\} }. \]

This equation explains the word total: the rate of one task component is the sum of the contributions from all changing joint coordinates. At a fixed configuration \(\mathbf q\), the relationship is linear in \(\dot{\mathbf q}\); as the robot changes configuration, the derivative matrix generally changes as well.

The same chain rule applies when one coordinate mapping feeds another. Let \(p\in\mathbb N\) be the number of upstream coordinates, let \(\mathbf r\in\mathbb R^p\) be an upstream coordinate column, let \(\mathbf q=\mathbf g(\mathbf r)\), and let \(\mathbf y=\mathbf f(\mathbf q)\). For example, \(\mathbf r\) could contain actuator coordinates and \(\mathbf g\) could describe a transmission that produces joint coordinates. Write \(g_j\) for component \(j\) of \(\mathbf g\), and let \(k\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\) be an upstream-coordinate index. The composed map is \(\mathbf y=(\mathbf f\circ\mathbf g)(\mathbf r)\). For output index \(i\) and upstream index \(k\),

\[ \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial r_k} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial q_j} \frac{\partial g_j}{\partial r_k}. \]

Collecting these scalar equations gives the matrix form

\[ \boxed{ \mathrm D(\mathbf f\circ\mathbf g)(\mathbf r) = \mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf g(\mathbf r)) \mathrm D\mathbf g(\mathbf r) }. \tag{10.5}\]

Here \(\mathrm D\mathbf g(\mathbf r)\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\) first maps an upstream increment into a joint-coordinate increment, and \(\mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf g(\mathbf r))\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) then maps that joint-coordinate increment into a task increment. Their product belongs to \(\mathbb R^{m\times p}\). The multiplication order therefore follows the information flow.

For the planar 2R position map Equation 10.1, differentiating each output with respect to each joint angle gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial x_e}{\partial\theta_1} &= -L_1\sin\theta_1 -L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2), & \frac{\partial x_e}{\partial\theta_2} &= -L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2),\\ \frac{\partial y_e}{\partial\theta_1} &= L_1\cos\theta_1 +L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2), & \frac{\partial y_e}{\partial\theta_2} &= L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2). \end{aligned} \]

The total-derivative matrix is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \mathrm D\mathbf f_{xy}(\mathbf q) = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1\sin\theta_1-L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ L_1\cos\theta_1+L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{bmatrix} }. \tag{10.6}\]

Each column is the end-effector position rate produced per unit rate of one joint while the other joint rate is zero. This particular total derivative will later be called the position-task Jacobian. Applying Equation 10.4 gives

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \dot x_e\\ \dot y_e \end{bmatrix} = \mathrm D\mathbf f_{xy}(\mathbf q) \begin{bmatrix} \dot\theta_1\\ \dot\theta_2 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

For example, let \(L_1=L_2=1\,\mathrm m\), \(\theta_1=0\), \(\theta_2=\pi/2\), and

\[ \dot{\mathbf q} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2\\-0.1 \end{bmatrix} \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}. \]

At this configuration,

\[ \mathrm D\mathbf f_{xy}(\mathbf q) = \begin{bmatrix} -1&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} \mathrm{m\,rad^{-1}}, \]

so the end-effector position rate is

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \dot x_e\\ \dot y_e \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.2\\-0.1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.1\\0.2 \end{bmatrix} \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}. \]

The units show the role of the derivative: multiplying metres per radian by radians per second produces metres per second. The result is the velocity of the selected end-effector origin expressed along the \(x_s\) and \(y_s\) axes; it is not the velocity of every point on the end-effector link.

For a pose-valued forward map \(\mathbf F_e:\mathcal Q\rightarrow SE(3)\), the output is constrained to \(SE(3)\) rather than being an unconstrained Euclidean column. Differentiating its matrix entries produces \(\dot{\mathbf T}_{se}\), but \(\dot{\mathbf T}_{se}\) is not itself a six-component rigid velocity. The established relations

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol\xi_s} = \dot{\mathbf T}_{se}\mathbf T_{se}^{-1}, \qquad \widehat{\boldsymbol\xi_e} = \mathbf T_{se}^{-1}\dot{\mathbf T}_{se} \]

convert the same pose rate into the space twist \(\boldsymbol\xi_s\), expressed using the fixed frame \(\{s\}\) and its origin, or the body twist \(\boldsymbol\xi_e\), expressed using the moving end-effector frame \(\{e\}\) and its origin. Both use the project’s linear-first component order. The later velocity-kinematics section will combine these relations with \(\dot{\mathbf q}\) to construct space and body robot Jacobians.

The derivative is local. It predicts first-order output change for a small coordinate increment and gives the exact instantaneous output rate along a differentiable motion; it does not by itself give an exact finite displacement, enforce joint limits, resolve angle-wrap boundaries, or prove that an inverse map exists. Equal input and output dimensions are also insufficient for local invertibility: the derivative must have the required rank, which will be studied in Section 10.4.

Quick test A: coordinates, forward maps, and total derivatives

  1. Recall: Distinguish \(\mathcal C\), \(\mathcal Q\), and one coordinate column \(\mathbf q\).
  2. Explanation: Why can two real angle values that differ by \(2\pi\) describe the same revolute-joint configuration, and why does this make coordinates local?
  3. Task choice: For one forward-pose map \(\mathbf F_e\), give two different task-coordinate maps and state what information each discards.
  4. Derivation: Starting from \(\mathbf y(t)=\mathbf f(\mathbf q(t))\), write the scalar multivariable chain rule for \(\dot y_i\) and its matrix form.
  5. Application: For the planar 2R example with \(L_1=L_2=1\,\mathrm m\), \(\theta_1=0\), \(\theta_2=\pi/2\), and \(\dot{\mathbf q}=[0.2\;-0.1]^{\mathsf T}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), calculate \([\dot x_e\;\dot y_e]^{\mathsf T}\) and state its frame and units.
  6. Composition: If actuator coordinates \(\mathbf r\) produce joint coordinates \(\mathbf q=\mathbf g(\mathbf r)\) and the task is \(\mathbf y=\mathbf f(\mathbf q)\), which derivative matrix multiplies an actuator-rate column \(\dot{\mathbf r}\)?
  7. Limitation: Why does the first-order approximation \(\Delta\mathbf y\approx\mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q)\Delta\mathbf q\) not provide an exact finite-motion formula or guarantee a local inverse?

Answers and hints

  1. \(\mathcal C\) is the set of complete admissible robot placements. \(\mathcal Q\) is the declared domain of their generalised-coordinate descriptions. \(\mathbf q\) is one coordinate column in \(\mathcal Q\); together with the model it locally specifies one configuration.
  2. A complete turn returns a revolute joint to the same relative orientation, so \(\theta\) and \(\theta+2\pi\) are different numerical representatives of one physical arrangement. Any one-to-one real coordinate must select a local interval or a branch boundary; it cannot unwrap the whole circle globally without duplication.
  3. The position map \(\mathbf f_p=\pi_p\circ\mathbf F_e\) returns \({}^{s}\mathbf p_e\) and discards orientation. A declared orientation-coordinate map would retain orientation and discard position. A full local pose-coordinate map can retain both, but its orientation coordinates require a declared representation and may have a representation boundary.
  4. For every output index \(i\), \(\dot y_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial f_i/\partial q_j)\dot q_j\). Collecting the components gives \(\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf q)\dot{\mathbf q}\).
  5. The derivative matrix is \([[-1,-1],[1,0]]\,\mathrm{m\,rad^{-1}}\), so the position rate is \([-0.1\;0.2]^{\mathsf T}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). It is the velocity of the selected end-effector origin expressed along the \(x_s\) and \(y_s\) axes.
  6. Apply the composition chain rule: \(\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathrm D\mathbf f(\mathbf g(\mathbf r))\mathrm D\mathbf g(\mathbf r)\dot{\mathbf r}\). The product \(\mathrm D\mathbf f\,\mathrm D\mathbf g\) is the derivative of the actuator-to-task map.
  7. The derivative retains only the linear term at the current configuration; nonlinear higher-order terms accumulate over a finite coordinate change. A local inverse additionally requires suitable dimensions and full rank, and it may still be restricted by coordinate boundaries and the physical domain.
